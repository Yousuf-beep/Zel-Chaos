## 1. What it is
- PCOS, now renamed PMOS (Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome), is a common, long-term hormonal and metabolic condition. It affects about 1 in 8 women worldwide. Indian studies report widely varying prevalence (roughly 9%–36% depending on population and criteria), and many cases go undiagnosed because of low awareness.
- On 12 May 2026, PCOS was officially renamed PMOS via a global consensus published in The Lancet; transition over 3 years, fully adopted in the 2028 International Guideline update. The name moves focus away from "cysts" toward hormones and metabolism.
- It is not mainly about cysts. The small "cysts" seen on ultrasound are follicles. Many people with the condition don't have them.
- It involves periods/ovulation, androgen-related skin and hair changes, metabolism (insulin resistance), and emotional wellbeing.
- There is no single test. Diagnosis is made by a doctor after ruling out other causes.

## 2. How doctors assess it (adults) — for explanation only; the app never applies it as a diagnosis
Doctors look for 2 of these 3, after excluding other causes:
1. Irregular cycles / ovulation problems.
2. Signs of high androgens: clinical (excess facial/body hair, acne, scalp hair thinning) or on blood tests.
3. Polycystic ovary appearance on ultrasound or raised AMH on a blood test (adults only). Neither is a standalone test.
If both 1 and 2 are present, ultrasound/AMH aren't needed to make the diagnosis.
The app can only observe features 1 and 2 from what a person reports, and must never claim to assess 3.

What counts as an irregular cycle (2023 guideline):
- In the first year after periods begin: irregular cycles are normal (part of puberty).
- 1 to <3 years after first period: cycles shorter than 21 or longer than 45 days.
- 3+ years after first period until perimenopause: cycles shorter than 21 or longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year.
- More than 1 year after first period: any single cycle longer than 90 days.
- Periods not started by age 15, or more than 3 years after breast development began: should be checked by a doctor.

## 3. Adolescents
- Adolescent assessment needs both irregular cycles (by the definitions above) and androgen signs.
- Ultrasound is not recommended for this purpose within 8 years of the first period. AMH is not recommended in adolescents.
- In the first year after periods start, irregular cycles are expected. Reassure and encourage tracking.
- Teens with some features but not both criteria may be described by doctors as "at risk" and reviewed again later.
- For users under 18: gentler language; suggest involving a trusted adult (parent, guardian, school nurse) alongside a doctor.

## 4. Symptom clusters
- Cycle: irregular, infrequent, or absent periods (definitions above); very long gaps.
- Androgen-related: excess hair on chin, upper lip, chest, abdomen, back; persistent/adult acne; scalp hair thinning.
- Metabolic: weight gain especially around the abdomen; dark, velvety skin patches on neck, underarms, groin (acanthosis nigricans); skin tags; family history of type 2 diabetes. Increased long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, gestational diabetes, and sleep apnoea.
- Fertility: difficulty conceiving (many people with PCOS/PMOS do conceive; some need medical support).
- Emotional wellbeing: anxiety, low mood, body-image distress are more common; the guideline recommends doctors check for these.

## 5. Conditions that can look similar (a doctor will consider these)
Pregnancy; thyroid disorders; raised prolactin; non-classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia; low weight, stress, or over-exercise affecting cycles (hypothalamic causes); premature ovarian insufficiency; rarely, androgen-secreting tumours (suspect with rapid new hair growth, voice deepening); rarely, Cushing's syndrome.

## 6. Tests a doctor may consider (never self-order)
Pregnancy test (first, if a period is missed and pregnancy is possible); thyroid (TSH); prolactin; androgen levels (e.g. testosterone); other hormones such as LH/FSH; metabolic checks (fasting glucose or HbA1c, cholesterol/lipids); pelvic ultrasound (adults only); AMH (adults only).

## 7. Specialist routing
- Gynaecologist: first stop for period problems and suspected PCOS/PMOS.
- Endocrinologist: metabolic or complex hormonal picture.
- Dermatologist: persistent acne, excess hair, hair thinning.
- General physician / PHC doctor: acceptable first stop when a specialist isn't accessible.
- Dietitian: lifestyle support (never suggest specific diets in-app).
- Mental health professional: if low mood or anxiety is prominent.
- Free / low-cost options (India): government hospitals and Primary Health Centres; eSanjeevani (national government teleconsultation service).

## 8. General self-care (non-prescriptive)
Regular movement you enjoy, balanced regular meals, enough sleep, stress management, and tracking periods and symptoms in a diary or app to show the doctor. No supplements, drugs, crash diets, or numbers.

## 9. Other common concerns (non-PCOS route)
- Burning/pain when passing urine, frequent urge: possible urinary infection pattern; see a doctor within 1–2 days; urgent if fever, back pain, or blood.
- Unusual discharge, itching, odour: gynaecologist or GP; urgent if with fever and pelvic pain.
- Very painful periods that disrupt daily life, pain during sex: worth discussing with a gynaecologist (conditions like endometriosis can cause this).
- Constant tiredness, breathlessness on mild effort, pale skin, heavy periods: worth checking for anaemia with a doctor.
- Hot flushes, night sweats, cycle changes in 40s: may relate to perimenopause; discuss with a doctor.
